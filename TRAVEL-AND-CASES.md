# Travel, Cases and Safes

Three different transport problems get solved with the same purchase — a box — and the box is usually the wrong one for two of them.

---

## Travel: what actually breaks watches

Ranked by how often it happens, not by how dramatic it is:

| Cause | Frequency | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| Crushed in luggage | Very common | Hard case, watch not loose in a pocket |
| Knocked against a door frame or desk while worn | Common | Nothing — this is normal wear |
| Strap cut or deformed by a tight travel roll | Common | Correct cushion diameter (see below) |
| Theft from checked luggage | Occasional | Never check a watch |
| Condensation from a cold hold then warm cabin | Occasional | Keep in the cabin, in a sealed bag |
| Lost in security trays | Occasional | Put it in your bag before the tray, not on it |
| Magnetism from a bag clasp | Rare | Compass check after travel |

`[JUDGEMENT]` **Never put a watch in checked luggage.** Not for the damage risk — for the theft risk and the fact that an airline's liability for valuables in checked baggage is typically near zero.

### The travel roll problem

A soft roll with a fixed-size cushion will do one of two things: if the cushion is too large, it stretches the strap closed and holds it there for days; if too small, the watch slides and the case knocks against the roll's interior.

The correct fit: the cushion should be **just large enough that the closed strap rests on it with no tension**, and the case should not move when the roll is shaken. If a roll came with your winder, check the fit with your largest and smallest watch — most rolls are sized for one and wrong for the other.

### In-flight

- **Cabin only**, in a case, inside your hand luggage rather than a coat pocket.
- A watch running on a winder at home is unaffected by your trip; a watch travelling with you will lose its power reserve if it is a low-power movement and you do not wear it. See [LONG-TERM-STORAGE](LONG-TERM-STORAGE.md) for whether that matters.
- **Do not wear a watch through a security scanner and then leave it in the tray.** This is where watches are lost, and it happens weekly at every large airport.

## Cases: soft, hard, and the ones that mark the watch

| Type | Good for | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|
| Soft zip pouch | One watch, in a bag | No crush protection; zip can scratch the crystal |
| Multi-slot soft case | Travel with 2–6 watches | Watches touch each other at the flanks |
| Hard case with foam | Travel, storage | Foam degrades and crumbles onto dials over years |
| Watch roll | Travel, short term | Wrong cushion size (above) |
| Display box with glass lid | Home display | Light exposure — see [STORAGE-ENVIRONMENT](STORAGE-ENVIRONMENT.md) |
| Individual hard pods | Storage, best protection | Space-inefficient |

`[JUDGEMENT]` Foam is the trap. Polyurethane foam used in cheap cases **degrades after 5–10 years and turns into a sticky dust** that settles on dials and into crown tubes. If a case has been in a loft for a decade, throw the foam away before putting a watch back in it.

## Safes: the heat and moisture problem

A safe is sold as security and is often a worse environment than the room it stands in.

**Moisture.** Safes are sealed. Water vapour from the room migrates in and has no route out. A safe in an unheated room can sit at 70% RH while the room reads 50%. Unpainted steel interiors and any foam lining make this worse.

`[JUDGEMENT]` Put a hygrometer inside the safe, not in the room. If it reads above 60%, add indicating silica gel and regenerate it monthly, or use a rechargeable dehumidifier unit rated for the volume.

**Heat.** A safe with a fire rating is designed to keep the interior below a threshold during a fire — typically by releasing moisture from its lining or by insulation. In normal use, that insulation also means **heat generated inside has nowhere to go**. Which brings us to the specific mistake:

### The winder-inside-the-safe mistake

Do not put a running watch winder inside a sealed safe. Three reasons, all real:

1. The motor and its power supply produce heat continuously. In a sealed insulated box, that heat accumulates.
2. Running a power cable into a fire-rated safe usually means compromising the seal or the door — which is the exact feature you paid for.
3. `[JUDGEMENT]` A winder that pauses only when the door opens is a winder you cannot inspect. Motor faults, cable wear and unusual noise go unnoticed for months.

If watches must live in a safe and must keep running, keep the winder outside and move watches in and out — or accept that a safe is for watches at rest.

## Bank safe deposit boxes

No power, no climate control, and access by appointment. Practical implications:

- Watches will stop. That is fine — see [LONG-TERM-STORAGE](LONG-TERM-STORAGE.md).
- Humidity varies by facility and is usually not disclosed. Ask, and bring a small hygrometer on your next visit.
- Do not store a watch there if you might want to wear it on short notice, unless you enjoy the reset ritual.
- `[JUDGEMENT]` Safe deposit boxes are excellent for **documents and records** — see [INSURANCE-AND-RECORDS](INSURANCE-AND-RECORDS.md) — and mediocre for watches.

## Shipping a watch

If you are sending a watch for service or sale:

1. **Remove the strap or bracelet** if it is detachable. It cannot then be crushed against the case, and it reduces the parcel's profile.
2. **Wrap the watch in soft material, then immobilise it** in a rigid box with padding that compresses. The goal is that nothing moves when the box is shaken.
3. **Double box** — the inner rigid box goes inside an outer carton with padding around it.
4. **Ship insured, with tracking, signature required.** Declare the value; under-declaring voids most claims.
5. **Photograph the watch and the packing** before closing. This is the only evidence you will have if a claim is disputed.
