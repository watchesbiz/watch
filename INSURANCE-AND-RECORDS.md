# Insurance and Records

Two audiences need the same evidence: your insurer, if the watch is lost, and a buyer, if you ever sell it. Building the record once serves both.

This page is about what to capture and how to store it — not about which policy to buy, which varies by country and by insurer.

---

## What insurers actually ask for

| Requirement | Why it matters | How often people fail it |
|---|---|---|
| **Proof of ownership** — receipt, invoice, or valuation | Establishes the watch is yours | Receipt lost, or the watch was a gift with no document |
| **Serial number** | Makes the item uniquely identifiable; prevents paying out on a different watch | Not recorded, or recorded from a photo where it is unreadable |
| **Photographs** — clear, multiple, dated | Evidence of condition and existence | One blurry phone photo from three years ago |
| **Independent valuation** for vintage or high-value pieces | Insurers will not accept your own estimate | Assumed the purchase price was enough |
| **Named-item schedule** for anything above the single-article limit | Otherwise a household policy caps the payout | The cap was never checked |

`[COMMON]` The single most common and most expensive mistake: a watch worth more than the policy's **single article limit** is covered only up to that limit. Standard household policies often cap single items at a low figure, and watches frequently exceed it. Either the item is scheduled separately, or it is under-insured and nobody notices until the claim.

## Capturing the serial number properly

Serial numbers are engraved, small, and often in a place that photographs badly.

**Where to look** (varies by brand — check yours):

- Between the lugs on one side of the case, often the 12 o'clock end
- On the caseback, sometimes under a sticker or on the inner flange
- On the movement (only visible with the caseback off)
- On the warranty card or certificate, which also carries the reference number

**How to photograph it** so it is legible: raking light from one side at a low angle, macro mode, no flash on axis. Take three attempts at different angles — engraved numbers photographed straight-on with flash are usually unreadable.

`[JUDGEMENT]` Store the serial number as **text**, not only as an image. If you ever need to give it to the police or an insurer over the phone, a photo on a phone that was also stolen is useless.

---

## The photograph set

`[JUDGEMENT]` Twelve photos, taken once a year, cover almost every claim and every resale question:

1. Full front, dial straight on, ref. no. visible on paperwork in the frame
2. Caseback with serial number legible
3. Both case flanks — crown side and the opposite side
4. Clasp, opened, showing any engraving
5. Bracelet or strap, full length
6. All four lugs (the most common impact damage site)
7. Crystal edge and bezel insert
8. Any existing scratches or dents, close up — **declare pre-existing damage up front**
9. The watch on a wrist, showing scale
10. Box, papers, warranty card together with the watch
11. The serial number, cropped and readable
12. A photo of the receipt or valuation document

Also photograph the watch **with a dated item in frame** — a newspaper, or the phone showing the date. Insurers rarely demand this; it resolves disputes instantly.

## Box and papers: the resale argument

`[COMMON]` A complete set — original box, warranty card, booklets, and the hang tag — commands a meaningful premium over a watch alone, and makes a private sale substantially easier. For modern watches the spread is commonly quoted in the low-to-mid double digits as a percentage.

The practical implication for storage: **the box is an asset, not packaging.** Store it dry, flat, and away from the watch. Cardboard and foam both absorb moisture and both attract pests in a loft. A box that has been damp for years smells of it, and buyers notice.

`[JUDGEMENT]` Keep the box and papers somewhere **different from the watch**. If the watch is stolen from your home, the box is unlikely to be taken with it — and the papers are what let you prove the watch was yours.

## The service record

The single most valuable document for both insurance and resale is a **service history**: who serviced the watch, when, what was replaced, and what the measured rate and amplitude were afterwards.

A watch with a documented service history sells faster and for more than an identical watch with none, because the buyer's risk is lower. This costs nothing to maintain — one page per watch, updated once every few years.

The template is in [MAINTENANCE-LOG-TEMPLATE](MAINTENANCE-LOG-TEMPLATE.md). Copy it and keep it with the photographs.

## Where to keep the record

Three copies, in three places, and at least one off-site:

| Copy | Format | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Cloud storage | Photos + a text file | Accessible if the house is lost; check it is not on a device you carry |
| Paper | Printed photographs, receipts, valuations | In a safe or a safe deposit box — see [TRAVEL-AND-CASES](TRAVEL-AND-CASES.md) |
| With the insurer | Declared schedule | Send them the photos when the policy is written, not after a loss |

Do not keep the only copy of the serial numbers on a phone that lives in the same bag as the watches.

## If a watch is lost or stolen

1. **Report to police and get a crime reference number** before calling the insurer. Most policies require it for theft.
2. **Notify the insurer within the policy's stated window** — often 24–48 hours, and often shorter than people assume.
3. **Give them the serial number, the photographs, and the valuation.** This is the moment the file you built either works or does not.
4. `[JUDGEMENT]` **Register the serial number with the manufacturer** where the brand offers it. A stolen serial that shows up at a service centre is the one route to recovery that actually works.
