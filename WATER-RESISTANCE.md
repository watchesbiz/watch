# Water Resistance: What the Number Means Once the Watch Is Old

The depth rating on a caseback is a **factory-new, static, laboratory** figure. It has an expiry date, it is not the same as a diving depth, and the most common way a watch floods has nothing to do with the rating at all.

---

## The rating translated into actual use

`[COMMON]` Industry-standard interpretation. The gap between the number and reality is because test pressure is applied statically, while a moving arm entering water creates brief dynamic pressure spikes far above depth-equivalent.

| Rating marked | Real capability | Do not |
|---|---|---|
| 30 m / 3 ATM / "water resistant" | Rain, hand washing, splashes | Shower, swim, submerge |
| 50 m / 5 ATM | Light swimming in still water, brief immersion | Snorkel, dive, jump in, high-pressure shower |
| 100 m / 10 ATM | Swimming, snorkelling, water sports at surface | Scuba, high-board entry, jet ski impact |
| 200 m / 20 ATM | Recreational scuba (with a screw-down crown) | Saturation diving, deep technical diving |
| 300 m+ / ISO 6425 "DIVER'S" | Professional diving, within the standard's test regime | Anything the manual excludes — helium valves have their own rules |

`[STD]` **ISO 22810** covers general water-resistant watches. **ISO 6425** is the stricter diving standard and is the only one that permits the word "DIVER'S" on the dial. A watch marked 200 m without ISO 6425 is water resistant, not a dive instrument.

---

## The rating is a new-watch figure

Three things degrade it, and none of them appear on the caseback.

### Gaskets

`[COMMON]` Elastomer gaskets — caseback O-ring, crown seals, crystal gasket, pusher seals — harden with age and with ozone exposure. Their sealing force drops gradually and then fails without warning. Practical working life is often quoted as **3–5 years of active use**, less for watches exposed to chlorine, sunscreen, and hot water.

This is why a 12-year-old 100 m watch is not a 100 m watch. It is a watch with a 100 m marking on it.

### Gasket compression set

A gasket held compressed for years takes a permanent set and stops springing back when the case is opened. **Every time the case is opened, the gasket should be replaced** — not inspected, replaced. The part costs a few pounds; the failure mode costs a movement.

### Crystal and case deformation

Impact that does not visibly crack a sapphire can still deform the case or unseat the crystal gasket. A watch that has had a hard knock is a candidate for a pressure test even if it looks fine.

---

## The failure modes, ranked by how often they actually happen

| Cause | Frequency | Preventable |
|---|---|---|
| **Crown not screwed down / pushed fully in** | Very common | Completely — this is a habit, not a fault |
| **Aged gaskets, watch never pressure-tested** | Common | Yes, by testing |
| **Hot water: shower, bath, sauna, hot tub** | Common | Completely — see below |
| **Pushers operated underwater** | Occasional | Read the manual; many chronographs forbid it |
| **Impact damage to case or crystal** | Occasional | Partly |
| **Defective from factory** | Rare | Pressure test on purchase |

### The hot water problem

`[JUDGEMENT]` No watch should be worn in a shower, sauna, hot tub, or bath, regardless of rating — including dive watches. The reason is not the water pressure but the **temperature**:

- Water vapour molecules are smaller than liquid water molecules and pass gaskets that liquid cannot.
- Heat expands the air inside the case; when the watch cools, that air contracts and draws moisture inward through the same path.

The result is condensation *inside* the crystal, which is the beginning of rust on steel components. A 300 m dive watch can flood in a hot shower while being perfectly fine at 300 m of seawater.

---

## Pressure testing: cheap, fast, do it

A watchmaker can pressure-test a watch in a few minutes with a dry or wet tester. It costs very little, and it is the only way to know whether the seals still seal.

`[JUDGEMENT]` Test when:

- The watch is more than 5 years old and has never been tested
- Any time the case has been opened, for any reason
- After a hard impact
- Before any trip involving swimming or diving
- Annually, if the watch is worn in water regularly

Ask for the result in **bar** or **ATM**, and what it was tested to. "Passed" without a number is not a test result.

---

## Vintage watches: assume nothing

`[JUDGEMENT]` A vintage watch with a 100 m marking should be treated as splash-resistant only, unless a watchmaker has replaced every gasket and pressure-tested it. Fifty-year-old gasket material has no sealing properties left, and vintage crystal gaskets are often unavailable or replaced with incorrect parts.

The same applies to any watch whose service history is unknown.
