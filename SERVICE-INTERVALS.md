# Service Intervals: Reading the Watch Instead of the Calendar

"Every five years" is the most repeated number in watch ownership and it is wrong in both directions — it sends healthy watches to the bench unnecessarily, and it lets worn ones run until a repair becomes a rebuild.

This page replaces the calendar with six things you can actually observe, and gives a threshold for each.

---

## Why there is no correct fixed interval

Three reasons, and they compound:

**1. Manufacturers disagree with each other.** Published recommendations currently range from roughly five years to roughly ten, for movements built to comparable standards. Some brands have lengthened their stated interval over the past decade as synthetics improved; others have kept five years, partly because an inspection is also a revenue event and a chance to catch water damage early.

**2. Oil life depends on use, not time.** A watch worn daily at high amplitude keeps its lubricant distributed; a watch that runs two weeks a year dries out differently. A watch stored in a 70% RH drawer ages its gaskets faster than one in a dry cabinet, regardless of either.

**3. A service is not risk-free.** Every service opens the case, disturbs gasket seating, and hand-refinishes parts. `[JUDGEMENT]` A watch running within ±5 s/day at good amplitude does not benefit from being opened. The correct interval is the shortest one that avoids wear, not the shortest one available.

## The six symptoms, with thresholds

Measure these with a timegrapher app (a phone microphone against the caseback is adequate for rate and amplitude on most movements) or a watchmaker's reading.

| # | Symptom | Healthy | Investigate | Act now |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Amplitude** (full wind, dial up) | 270–315° | 240–270° | Below 240° |
| 2 | **Rate spread across positions** | under 15 s/day | 15–30 s/day | over 30 s/day |
| 3 | **Power reserve vs. spec** | 95%+ of rated | 80–95% | under 80% |
| 4 | **Winding feel** | Smooth, even resistance | Slight roughness | Gritty, or slips |
| 5 | **Stops overnight** | Never | Occasionally | Routinely |
| 6 | **Rate drift over 6 months** | under 3 s/day change | 3–8 s/day | over 8 s/day |

A low amplitude with a normal rate is the most commonly missed signal: the watch still tells time correctly, but it is running on a thin oil film and the pivots are the parts paying for it. Amplitude falls before rate does.

`[JUDGEMENT]` **Amplitude below 240° is the trigger, not rate error.** Waiting for the watch to visibly misbehave means waiting until wear has started, at which point the service involves replacing pivots or a wheel train rather than cleaning and re-oiling.

## The decision table

| Your situation | Action |
|---|---|
| All six green, watch under 8 years old | Do nothing. Re-measure in 12 months |
| All six green, watch over 10 years old, never serviced | Have a watchmaker measure amplitude and listen to it — a bench check, not a service |
| One amber item | Book a service within the next few months. Do not rush, do not ignore |
| Two or more amber, or any red | Service now |
| Any water ingress, condensation under the crystal, or a fall | Service immediately, regardless of all other readings. Rust inside a case progresses in days |
| Watch stopped for 12+ months, now restarting | Full measurement before wearing. A long layoff is when dried pivots are discovered |

## What a service actually includes

Worth knowing, because "service" and "service" are not the same quote:

| Included | Usually | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Full disassembly, cleaning, reassembly | Yes | The core of the work |
| Fresh lubricants at all friction points | Yes | The reason the service exists |
| New gaskets (caseback, crown, crystal, pushers) | Usually | Ask explicitly; not always automatic |
| Case and bracelet refinishing | Often optional | Refinishing removes metal — some collectors decline |
| Mainspring replacement | Conditional | Replaced if set or if power reserve was low |
| Crystal replacement | Conditional | Only if chipped or delaminated |
| Regulation and rate testing | Yes | Should come with a printed reading |

Ask for the **before-and-after amplitude and rate**, in writing. A watchmaker who cannot produce those numbers did not measure the watch. This also builds the record you need for resale — see [INSURANCE-AND-RECORDS](INSURANCE-AND-RECORDS.md).

## The three ways people make it worse

**"Just regulate it."** Adjusting the regulator to make a low-amplitude watch keep time does not add oil. It hides the symptom and accelerates the wear, because the watch is now being pushed to perform on dry pivots. If a watchmaker offers to regulate without measuring amplitude, find another watchmaker.

**Doing it yourself with the wrong lubricant.** Household oils, WD-40, and "watch oil" sold without a viscosity grade are the fastest way to convert a £300 service into a £900 rebuild. Watch oils are graded and applied in quantities measured in fractions of a microlitre.

**Skipping the gaskets to save money.** The gaskets are a small fraction of the cost and the entire defence against the failure mode that actually destroys movements. If the case is open, replace them.

## Intervals by movement type, as a sanity check

`[COMMON]` Real-world ranges, not manufacturer promises. Use the symptom table above as the primary guide.

| Movement type | Typical real interval | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Modern in-house automatic, daily wear | 7–10 years | Synthetics, good sealing, high amplitude in use |
| Modern automatic, occasional wear | 8–12 years | Fewer running hours, but oil sits |
| Vintage (pre-1980) manual or automatic | 4–6 years | Older oils, weaker sealing, more wear tolerance in the design |
| Chronograph with a module (Dubois-Dépraz type) | 5–8 years | More friction points, module often needs separate attention |
| Perpetual calendar / high complication | 5–8 years, by a specialist | Not a general watchmaker's job; document everything |
